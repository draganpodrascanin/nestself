## Why this conversation feels hard

Most people know when their workload is too much. Fewer feel comfortable saying so. The reasons are understandable: you worry about looking incapable, you do not want to seem like you are complaining, or you fear it will count against you.

But staying silent while the pressure builds leads to worse outcomes -- for you and for your work. A direct, well-prepared conversation is almost always better than quietly struggling.

## Before you say anything: prepare

Walking in and saying "I have too much work" rarely lands well. A little preparation turns a complaint into a problem-solving conversation.

### Map out what is on your plate

Write down every task and commitment you are responsible for right now. Include the recurring ones people forget about -- the weekly report, the inbox you triage, the onboarding you help with. Seeing it on paper often surprises people. It will also help your manager understand the full picture.

### Identify what is causing the strain

Is it the volume? The deadlines? Unclear priorities? Interruptions? Work that falls outside your role? Be specific. "I am struggling" is hard for a manager to act on. "I have three overlapping deadlines next week and no clarity on which one matters most" is something they can work with.

### Think about what would help

Come with ideas, even rough ones. Could a deadline move? Could someone else take on one task? Could a recurring meeting become fortnightly? You do not need to have the answer, but showing that you have thought about solutions changes the tone of the conversation.

<Callout kind="tip">
Frame the conversation around the work, not around how you feel. "These three projects overlap next week and I want to make sure I deliver the right one first" is easier for a manager to act on than "I feel overwhelmed." You can share how you feel too -- but lead with the practical problem.
</Callout>

## How to open the conversation

Ask for a short, private meeting. You can say something like:

"I would like 15 minutes to talk through my current workload. I want to make sure I am focusing on the right things."

This signals that you are being proactive, not complaining. It also gives your manager time to listen properly instead of responding on the fly.

## During the conversation

- **Start with what you are working on.** Walk through your list. Let them see the full picture.
- **Name the specific problem.** "These three things all need to be done by Friday and I can only do two well."
- **Ask for their priorities.** "Which of these matters most to you this week?"
- **Suggest an option.** "If I push the report to next Monday, I can finish the client deck properly."
- **Listen.** Your manager may have context you do not. They may also not realise how much is on your plate.

## If the conversation does not go well

Not every manager responds helpfully the first time. If yours dismisses the concern, gives vague reassurance, or adds more work, that is information too. Give it a few days and try again, or ask a colleague or HR representative for advice.

If the problem persists and the workload is affecting your health, it is worth speaking to someone outside the situation who can help you think through your options.
