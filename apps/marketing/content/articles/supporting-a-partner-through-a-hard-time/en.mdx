## When the person closest to you is struggling

It might be work stress, a health scare, a family conflict or something they cannot quite name. Whatever the cause, watching your partner go through a hard time is difficult. You want to fix it. You want to say the right thing. You worry about making it worse.

There is no script for this. But there are some things that consistently help – and some well-intentioned habits that do not.

## What helps

### Listen more than you advise

When someone is struggling, the most common instinct is to offer solutions. But most people in pain are not looking for a fix – they are looking to feel heard.

Try this: when your partner shares something difficult, pause before responding. Ask "do you want me to listen, or do you want help thinking through it?" That one question changes the entire conversation.

### Be present, not perfect

You do not need to have the right words. Sitting together in silence, making dinner, going for a walk without talking about the problem – these count. Presence is not about performance. It is about being there.

<Callout kind="tip">
Small, consistent gestures often matter more than grand ones. Making tea without being asked, handling a chore they usually do, sending a short message during the day – these things say "I see you" without pressure.
</Callout>

### Keep your routine together

When things are hard, routines are an anchor. Eating meals together, going to bed at the same time, keeping your weekend walk – these small patterns create stability when everything else feels uncertain.

You do not need to pretend everything is normal. But keeping some structure helps both of you.

### Ask what they need (and believe the answer)

People process difficulty differently. Your partner might need space. They might need closeness. They might need you to handle the groceries for a week. Ask – and when they tell you, take them at their word.

Avoid assuming you know what they need based on what you would need in their place. You are different people.

### Name what you see, gently

Sometimes the person struggling does not realise how much it is showing. You might notice they are sleeping more, eating less, withdrawing from friends, or snapping at small things.

Saying "I have noticed you seem more tired lately – how are you feeling?" opens a door without pushing. It is an observation, not a diagnosis.

## What does not help (even though it comes from love)

- **Trying to fix the problem for them.** Unless they ask for direct help, jumping to solutions can feel dismissive.
- **Comparing their situation to others.** "At least it is not as bad as..." minimises what they are going through.
- **Taking it personally.** If they are withdrawn or irritable, it is usually not about you. Give them room.
- **Putting your own needs on hold indefinitely.** Supporting someone does not mean ignoring yourself. You cannot pour from an empty cup.

## Taking care of yourself too

This is not selfish – it is necessary. Supporting someone through a hard time is emotionally demanding, and if you run yourself into the ground, neither of you benefits.

Keep seeing your own friends. Keep doing things that restore your energy. Talk to someone about how you are feeling – a friend, a family member, or a professional.

## When professional support would help

If your partner's difficult period lasts more than a few weeks and is affecting their daily life – or yours – professional support can make a real difference. A psychologist can help them work through what they are facing in a structured, confidential way.

Where your employer's plan includes family members, your partner can access the same therapy, legal and financial support.
