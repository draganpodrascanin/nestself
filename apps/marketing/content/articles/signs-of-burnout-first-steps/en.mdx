## It does not happen overnight

Burnout rarely arrives as a single dramatic moment. It creeps in over weeks or months: you sleep badly, you dread Monday, you feel flat even about things you used to enjoy. By the time you notice it, the pattern is already well established.

Understanding what burnout actually looks like – and what separates it from a rough week – is the first step toward doing something about it.

## What burnout feels like in practice

You might recognise some of these in yourself or in someone you work with:

- **Exhaustion that rest does not fix.** A weekend off still leaves you drained. Sleep feels shallow or broken.
- **Cynicism about work.** Tasks that once felt meaningful now feel pointless. You catch yourself thinking "what is even the point?"
- **Dropping effectiveness.** You spend more hours at your desk but produce less. Concentration is harder. Small decisions feel heavy.
- **Physical signals.** Headaches, tight shoulders, stomach trouble, getting ill more often than usual.
- **Withdrawal.** You skip lunch with colleagues, avoid calls, or stop replying to messages that are not urgent.

None of these on its own means burnout. But when several show up together and stay for weeks, your body and mind are telling you something.

<Callout kind="note">
Burnout is not a personal failure. It usually reflects a mismatch between what your job demands and the resources you have – time, support, autonomy, recovery. Recognising it early is a sign of self-awareness, not weakness.
</Callout>

## Five things you can do this week

You do not need to overhaul your life. Start with one or two of these and build from there.

### 1. Name what is draining you

Write down the three things at work that cost you the most energy right now. Be specific: "the weekly status report that takes four hours because three teams never send their numbers" is more useful than "too much work." Naming a problem makes it easier to act on.

### 2. Protect one recovery block each day

Block 30 minutes in your calendar for something that is not work and not a screen. A walk, a meal away from your desk, stretching, reading. Treat it like a meeting you cannot cancel. Recovery is not a luxury; it is maintenance.

### 3. Set one boundary

Pick the boundary that would make the biggest difference. It might be "no emails after 19:00" or "I will not take calls during lunch." Tell the people who need to know, and hold it for a week. One boundary is easier to keep than five.

### 4. Talk to someone you trust

This does not have to be a therapist – though it can be. A friend, a partner, a colleague. Say what you have been feeling out loud. Hearing yourself describe it often makes the next step clearer.

## When the signs have been there for a while

If you have been feeling exhausted, detached and ineffective for more than a few weeks, that is worth taking seriously. The longer burnout continues without a change, the harder it becomes to recover.

Small steps help. But sometimes you need a conversation with someone trained to help you work through it – someone outside your daily life who can see the pattern clearly.
