## The meeting nobody looks forward to

In most companies, one-on-ones follow the same pattern: a quick "how is everything going?", a run through the task list, a few nods, and a mutual sense that the meeting could have been an email.

It does not have to be this way. A well-run one-on-one is the most powerful tool a manager has for building trust, spotting problems early, and helping people grow. The difference is not about charisma – it is about structure and attention.

## Why one-on-ones matter

A one-on-one is the only regular time you and your direct report have that is not about a project, a deadline or a team decision. It is their time. When it works, it is where they tell you things they would never say in a group meeting – that they are struggling, that they want to move into a different role, that a process is broken, that they are thinking of leaving.

When it does not work, you lose access to all of that.

## Five practices that make the difference

### 1. Make it their meeting, not yours

The most common mistake managers make is using the one-on-one as a status check. You already have stand-ups, project boards and Slack for that.

Instead, let your direct report set the agenda. Ask them to bring one or two things they want to talk about. If they show up with nothing, that is information too – it usually means they do not feel safe raising topics, or the meeting has not earned their trust yet.

<Callout kind="tip">
A simple shared document with a running agenda works well. Both sides add topics before the meeting. Over time, it becomes a record of what you discussed, what you agreed, and what you followed up on.
</Callout>

### 2. Start with a real question

Skip "how are you?" – it almost always gets "fine." Try something specific:

- "What was the best part of your week?"
- "What is taking more energy than it should right now?"
- "Is there anything you have been putting off that I could help with?"
- "How are you feeling about the project deadline?"

These are not trick questions. They are invitations to be honest. Ask one, then listen.

### 3. Listen longer than feels comfortable

When someone shares something difficult – workload, a conflict, a personal issue affecting their work – the instinct is to respond immediately. Resist it. Let them finish. Ask a follow-up question. Then respond.

Most people can tell when you are genuinely listening and when you are waiting for your turn to speak. The difference changes everything.

### 4. Follow up on what you said you would do

Nothing destroys trust faster than forgetting. If you said you would look into something, check with HR, or raise a concern with your own manager – do it, and tell them what happened next time.

Keep a short list after each one-on-one: three things at most. Review it before the next meeting. This alone puts you ahead of most managers.

### 5. Protect the time

Cancel a one-on-one and you send a clear message: this is not important. Reschedule if you must, but never let it quietly drop off the calendar.

If you manage five people, that is two and a half hours a week in one-on-ones. It is the highest-return investment of your time as a manager.

## When someone is struggling

Sometimes a one-on-one reveals that your direct report is going through something bigger than work – stress at home, mental health difficulties, a family crisis. You do not need to be a therapist. You need to listen, take it seriously, and point them toward the right support.
