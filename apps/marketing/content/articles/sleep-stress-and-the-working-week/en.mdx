## The loop between stress and sleep

When work is stressful, sleep suffers. When sleep suffers, everything feels harder – decisions, patience, focus. Then the next day at work is worse, and the cycle tightens.

You do not need perfect sleep to feel better. But understanding how stress disrupts rest – and what you can realistically change – makes a real difference.

## How stress gets in the way of rest

Stress keeps your nervous system on alert. Your body produces more cortisol and adrenaline, which are useful in a genuine emergency but unhelpful at midnight when you are trying to fall asleep.

Common patterns people describe:

- **Racing thoughts at bedtime.** You replay the day or rehearse tomorrow's problems.
- **Falling asleep fine but waking at 3 a.m.** Your body drops out of deep sleep too early.
- **Feeling tired but wired.** You are exhausted, but your mind will not switch off.
- **Weekend catch-up that does not work.** Sleeping in on Saturday does not repay the week's debt the way you hope.

<Callout kind="tip">
If you find yourself checking your phone in bed, try leaving it in another room for one week. Most people who try this report falling asleep faster within three or four nights. The alarm still works from the hallway.
</Callout>

## Seven things that actually help

These are not dramatic lifestyle changes. They are small adjustments you can try one at a time.

### 1. Keep your wake-up time steady

Your body clock anchors to when you wake up more than when you go to bed. Pick a time and stick to it every day, including weekends. Give it two weeks before judging.

### 2. Stop working at a fixed time

Choose a hard stop – say, 18:30 – and close your laptop. If something urgent comes up, write it down for tomorrow. The act of closing the laptop is a signal to your brain that the workday is over.

### 3. Build a wind-down buffer

Give yourself 60 to 90 minutes between your last screen and sleep. This does not have to be meditation or a bath (though those are fine). Reading, cooking, stretching or talking to someone you live with all work. The point is to lower the tempo.

### 4. Move your body during the day

Even a 20-minute walk at lunch reduces the cortisol that keeps you alert at night. Exercise earlier in the day helps more than exercise close to bedtime.

### 5. Watch the caffeine cut-off

Caffeine stays in your system for six to eight hours. If you go to bed at 23:00, your last coffee should be before 15:00. Tea counts too.

### 6. Write down tomorrow's first task

Before you leave your desk, write one sentence: "Tomorrow I will start with ___." This offloads the planning from your sleeping brain. It sounds too simple to work, but it often does.

### 7. Keep the bedroom cool, dark and boring

Cool air (around 18 degrees Celsius), blackout curtains or a sleep mask, and no screens in the room. Your bedroom should signal sleep, not work.

## When a rough patch lasts longer than a few weeks

Everyone has bad stretches. A deadline, a conflict at work, a difficult situation at home – these disrupt sleep temporarily and that is normal.

But if you have been sleeping badly for more than three or four weeks, and it is affecting how you feel or function during the day, it is worth talking to someone about it. Poor sleep that lingers can worsen anxiety and low mood, and the cycle becomes harder to break on your own.
